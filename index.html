Medicine lists & monthly reminders

Monthly monitoring checklist

This page doesn't monitor on its own — run these steps monthly for each medicine. A scheduled assistant task can do this for you if you share your med list.

  1. Confirm identity. Read the NDC and labeler from your label; look the product up in the FDA NDC Directory (or the live check above).
  2. Find the factory. Enter your drug + labeler (or NDC) in ProPublica's Rx Inspector — it links NDCs to registered manufacturing facilities.
  3. Check inspection history. Rx Inspector shows the facility's FDA inspection timeline; FDA inspection classifications explain what NAI/VAI/OAI mean. A gap in public records is an information flag, not proof a site is uninspected or unsafe.
  4. Check for new recalls. Use the live check above or the openFDA enforcement API. Confirm scope: an old or unrelated-lot recall doesn't affect your bottle. Recall class definitions.
  5. Check therapeutic equivalence. Look up the TE code in the Orange Book.
  6. Update your med list whenever a label shows a new maker — and note the lot number so recalls can be checked against it.

For researchers: ProPublica publishes the underlying NDC-to-factory data on GitHub (Creative Commons, noncommercial with attribution).

GeneriCheck is an educational prototype that adapts the published scoring model of the Yuka app (weighted criteria, hazard caps, same-category alternatives) to generic-drug manufacturer quality signals. Yuka is not affiliated with this prototype; no Yuka code or data was used. Regulatory definitions are drawn from the FDA's inspection classifications, FDA recall classes, and the Orange Book preface; medication education from MedlinePlus.

How the score works

Each fictional product is graded on six quality signals, each 0–100. The index is their weighted sum, capped by hard barriers — the same shape as Yuka's published food model (60% nutrition, 30% additives, 10% organic, with hazard caps), applied here to manufacturer track records. This is an illustrative teaching index, not an FDA or WHO rating, and not a measure of clinical safety or effectiveness.

Scoring criteria and weights
SignalWeightWhat it reflects
Inspection record30%Recent FDA outcomes: NAI (no action), VAI (voluntary fixes), OAI (regulatory action)
Recall history25%FDA recall classes I (most serious), II, III
Batch consistency20%Simulated teaching value: potency and dissolution variability across batches
Supply reliability15%Shortage history — relevant because switches between makers are common
Quality transparency10%Voluntarily published quality data

Therapeutic-equivalence status (the Orange Book code, e.g. AB) is shown as a categorical badge — deliberately not scored. An AB rating is a yes/no determination, not a graduated efficacy ranking, so it cannot honestly contribute points.

Barrier caps

Only active, product-level events cap the index: a Class I recall, an OAI inspection, or an import alert cap it at 24; an active Class II recall or an open NTI bioequivalence inquiry caps it at 49. Resolved events and labeler-wide context inform the track-record criteria but never cap. Unassessed signals are excluded and weights are re-normalized — which is not strictly neutral (excluding a low signal can raise the index) — so coverage is always shown beside the score.

Badges

  • 70–100 — Strong quality signals
  • 40–69 — Mixed quality signals
  • 0–39 — Serious quality concerns

Identity and evidence

The NDC confirms the product and labeler — not the factory. Maker identity needs facility-level records (Rx Inspector links NDCs to registered factories); when records can't establish it, the answer is "unconfirmed", never a guess. A list of a company's plants doesn't prove which made your lot, and single-plant production is a supply-chain fact, not a quality verdict.

Live vs. mock boundary

The live check fetches real records from the FDA's openFDA API and never scores or ranks them. "No matching records" means none found in that query — not "no recalls exist". Findings are scoped to product, facility, lots, and dates; applicability to your medicine is confirmed, possible, or unconfirmed.

Alternatives

Below 60, the app lists up to three labelers of the same active ingredient, strength, and form with higher scores. They are candidates to discuss with your pharmacist — never instructions to switch on your own. Substitution rules vary by state and by drug (especially narrow-therapeutic-index drugs like levothyroxine and warfarin, where a manufacturer switch may prompt a TSH or INR recheck).
